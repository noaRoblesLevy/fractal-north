Funding on perpetual futures is one of the few cash flows in crypto that is paid on a
fixed schedule regardless of direction. Short a perpetual while funding is positive
and you are paid every eight hours. The systematic way to collect it is a
cross-sectional book: short the coins whose funding is highest, long the coins whose
funding is lowest, and receive the spread. It is the crypto cousin of the carry trades
found across every asset class.

I built one, backtested it on two venues, and ran it live as a shadow ledger. The
backtest showed a Sharpe ratio of 1.32 on Binance and 1.24 on Bybit. Live, it lost
15.3% in 153 days. This post explains why, and the answer is more useful than "the
backtest was wrong".

## The strategy

The universe is 23 liquid USDT-margined perpetuals. Every seven days the coins are
ranked on their latest funding rate. The book goes long the 11 lowest and short the 11
highest, each leg with a gross exposure of one:

$$
w_{i,t} =
\begin{cases}
  +1/N & i \in L_t \\
  -1/N & i \in S_t
\end{cases}
\qquad N = 11.
$$

With funding rate $f_{i,t}$, a position $w_i$ earns $-w_i f_{i,t}$ per period, so the
book's funding income is the cross-sectional spread between the two groups.

The catch is that high funding is not random. It marks coins that leveraged traders
are crowding into, typically the most volatile ones with the strongest momentum.
Shorting them imports a large price exposure, which the book offsets with a beta hedge
in BTC. Written generally, for a set of hedge instruments $F$ with covariance matrix
$\Sigma_F$:

$$
\mathbf{h} = -\Sigma_F^{-1}\,\mathbf{e},
\qquad
e_k = \sum_i w_i \operatorname{Cov}(r_i, F_k).
$$

With $F = \{\text{BTC}\}$ this is the familiar $h = -\sum_i w_i \beta_i$.

The key tool in everything that follows is a decomposition of each period's return
into a **carry** leg (funding received or paid) and a **price** leg (mark-to-market of
the coins and the hedge).

## What happened live

The live test is a shadow ledger: no orders, but live Bybit prices and the funding
events that actually printed, marked and accrued by a scheduled job from 6 June to
6 November 2026.

**The carry held up.** Live funding income annualised to 6.0%, close to the 7.1% the
same configuration earned in the Bybit backtest. The cash flow is real and accrues as
modelled.

**The price leg was 31 times more volatile than the carry**, with four steps above 4%.
A market-neutral book should not do that.

### It was not BTC

The obvious suspect is a hedge that failed. So I regressed each step's price P&L on the
BTC return over the same interval.

The slope is 0.06 and the $R^2$ is **1%**. Adding ETH raises it to 2%. The worst
step, ending 21 September, came while BTC rose 10.1%: the hedge made money, but the
shorted high-funding coins rallied far harder. The second worst came with BTC at
−1.8%. The hedge did exactly its job. What was left is the dispersion of individual
coins around the market, and no hedge in BTC, or in BTC and ETH, can touch that by
construction.

### Is the loss significant?

The standard error of an annualised Sharpe ratio over $T$ years is roughly
$\sqrt{(1 + \mathrm{SR}^2/2)/T}$. Over 153 days, against the Bybit backtest's 1.24,
that is 2.05, and the live Sharpe of −1.35 gives $z = -1.26$. Formally, five months
cannot reject the backtest.

The empirical view is less comfortable. Of all 153-day windows in the backtests, 29%
(Binance) and 23% (Bybit) lost money. But a loss of 15.3% or more happened in only 3%
of Binance windows and in **none** of the Bybit windows. Overlapping windows hold only
a handful of independent observations, so both views can be true at once: not
statistically decisive, yet at or beyond the edge of anything the backtest produced.

On its own the carry leg has a Sharpe ratio of 22.9 on Binance and 11.2 on Bybit, with
volatility under 1% a year. (These overstate what is attainable, because funding is
persistent and the annualisation assumes independent periods. The point survives any
reasonable correction.) The carry accounts for less than 0.1% of the variance of the
book. The price leg is everything else, with 25 to 30% annual volatility and a
correlation with BTC of essentially zero.

The backtests looked good because that price leg happened to be **profitable**: 33.9%
a year on Binance and 28.8% on Bybit, about three times the carry. And that profit is
concentrated in time:

| | Binance | Bybit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total price P&L | 147% | 81% |
| Best 90-day window | Apr to Jul 2021 | Nov 2024 to Feb 2025 |
| Share of total in that window | 62% | 78% |
| Largest contributors | SAND +16%, AXS +15% | DOT +13%, COMP +10% |

On Bybit, one 90-day window delivered 78% of the price leg's profit over almost three
years. A backtest whose return depends this much on two or three episodes has far more
sampling error than its Sharpe ratio suggests, and a live window without such an
episode should be expected to do much worse than the average.

There is a story in which this is a real premium: high funding marks crowded leveraged
longs, crowded positions unwind, so high-funding coins underperform. The data are too
short and too concentrated to tell that premium apart from a few lucky episodes, and I
don't claim either.

## Do better weights or a second factor help?

Two refinements are natural. **Inverse-volatility weights**: within each leg,
$w_i \propto 1/\sigma_i$, so the volatile short leg no longer dominates the risk.
**A second hedge factor**: ETH alongside BTC, using the matrix form above. I also
tested re-hedging every period. All variants trade exactly the same periods after a
35-day warm-up.

| Variant | Binance SR | Bybit SR | Binance max DD | Bybit max DD |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Baseline: 1/N, BTC | 1.32 | 1.24 | −32.1% | −22.8% |
| A: inverse vol, BTC | 1.46 | 1.12 | −29.5% | −19.1% |
| B: 1/N, BTC + ETH | 1.28 | 0.99 | −23.6% | −21.6% |
| C: inverse vol, BTC + ETH | 1.48 | 0.90 | −20.5% | −20.7% |
| D: C, re-hedged every period | 1.41 | 0.79 | −20.0% | −21.2% |

Every refinement lowers drawdown and price-leg volatility on both venues. Re-hedging
every period never improves the Sharpe ratio, so the weekly hedge is not stale. But the
effect on the Sharpe ratio **flips sign**: variant C lifts it from 1.32 to 1.48 on
Binance and cuts it from 1.24 to 0.90 on Bybit, the venue of the live test.

The refinements reduce the dispersion exposure, and whether that helps depends on
whether the removed exposure happened to pay in that sample. They also cannot remove
much of it: price-leg volatility falls only 13 to 16%, because BTC and ETH do not span
the idiosyncratic moves of the other 21 coins. Five variants on two samples, +0.16 on
one and −0.34 on the other, is evidence of a modest risk reduction, not of a better
strategy. Picking the variant that looks best in one sample would be textbook
selection bias.

<Callout title="A correction from my own first pass">
  My first comparison had variant C at a Sharpe of 1.33 against a baseline of 1.08.
  It was biased: the inverse-volatility variants sat flat through an extreme first
  week of January 2021 that the baseline traded. With a common warm-up the bias
  disappears, and on Bybit the conclusion reverses.
</Callout>

## Where the carry actually comes from

This is the most useful number in the study. Split by leg, the short side earned
**14.6%** a year on Binance and **9.7%** on Bybit. The long side earned −1.7% and
+0.7%. The long leg contributes nothing to the carry. It is only there to offset the
short leg's price risk, and it does that badly because the two sides hold different
coins.

That points to a different construction: short the high-funding perpetuals and hold
the **same** coins on spot, the classic cash-and-carry. Each position is then
delta-neutral by construction, and the price leg shrinks to the spot-perpetual basis
of one coin instead of the dispersion between 22. It removes the dispersion bet rather
than hedging it with market factors. It has its own costs (spot capital, two account
types, basis risk around funding resets) and I have not tested it yet. It is the next
experiment.

## Limitations

- **Short samples**: 4.3 and 2.8 years of backtest, five months live, with returns
  dominated by a few episodes.
- **Shadow ledger**: live P&L is marked to last prices with no transaction costs, so
  the live figures are if anything optimistic.
- **Gaps**: the scheduled job only runs while its host is on. One gap lasted 23 days,
  during which the book was not rebalanced.
- **Fixed universe**: the 23 coins were chosen with hindsight from coins that are
  still liquid, which flatters the backtest.
- **Multiple testing**: the variants were designed after seeing the live loss, which
  is why all of them are reported on both venues.

## Conclusion

The book collects a real, stable, near-riskless cash flow and wraps it in a dispersion
bet about thirty times more volatile. The BTC hedge neutralises the market almost
perfectly and is irrelevant to the dispersion, which drove the live loss. Better
weights and a second factor lower the risk a little but do not reliably raise the
return. To harvest the carry, the book has to stop holding different coins on its two
sides.

A second live ledger now runs variant C next to the unchanged baseline. The prior from
the table above is lower volatility, not higher return, and the number to watch is
price-leg volatility per step, which will be measurable long before returns mean
anything.

_This is research, not investment advice. No real capital was traded._
