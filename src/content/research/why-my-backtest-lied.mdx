A backtest is a hypothesis dressed as evidence. This note walks through a
concrete case where the costume was convincing: an equity curve so smooth it
looked like a bond ladder, a reported Sharpe above 3, and a live track record
that erased the illusion within a quarter.

## Setup

The strategy traded a liquid futures contract on a 15 minute bar. It combined a
handful of momentum and mean reversion signals, selected by a grid search over
lookback windows, entry thresholds, and a volatility filter. The search space
was large: roughly ten thousand parameter combinations were evaluated against
six years of history.

Nothing about the setup is unusual. That is the point. The failure mode is
structural, not exotic.

## What the backtest showed

On paper the results were excellent. Annualised return sat near 22 percent,
drawdowns stayed shallow, and the headline number was a Sharpe ratio of

$$
\mathrm{SR} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[r_t] - r_f}{\sigma(r_t)} \approx 3.1,
$$

computed on daily aggregated returns $r_t$ with a risk free rate $r_f$ set to
zero over the sample. A Sharpe above 3 on a single liquid instrument should
trigger suspicion immediately. Few real, capacity constrained strategies clear
2 out of sample, and those that do rarely survive contact with changing market
structure.

Here is the smell test I now run before anything else, counting how many
independent configurations were tried:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

def deflated_sharpe(sr_hat, n_obs, n_trials, skew=0.0, kurt=3.0):
    """Probability the true Sharpe exceeds zero, after accounting
    for the number of configurations searched (Bailey & Lopez de Prado)."""
    # Expected maximum Sharpe from n_trials pure-noise strategies.
    emc = 0.5772156649
    z1 = norm.ppf(1 - 1.0 / n_trials)
    z2 = norm.ppf(1 - 1.0 / (n_trials * np.e))
    sr0 = np.sqrt(1.0 / n_obs) * ((1 - emc) * z1 + emc * z2)

    # Variance of the Sharpe estimator under non-normal returns.
    se = np.sqrt((1 - skew * sr_hat + (kurt - 1) / 4 * sr_hat**2) / (n_obs - 1))
    return norm.cdf((sr_hat - sr0) / se)

print(deflated_sharpe(sr_hat=3.1 / np.sqrt(252), n_obs=252 * 6, n_trials=10_000))
```

## Live results

Deployed with real capital and real fills, the same logic produced a Sharpe
near 0.2, inside the noise band of "indistinguishable from flat." The equity
curve that had looked like a staircase in the backtest looked like a random
walk live. No single day blew up. The edge simply was not there.

## Diagnosis: overfitting and regime dependence

Two forces combined.

**Selection under multiplicity.** When you search ten thousand configurations,
the best one is partly skill and mostly luck. The maximum of many noisy
estimates is biased upward, and the bias grows with the number of trials. The
grid did not find an edge; it found the corner of the parameter space where
noise happened to align.

**Regime dependence.** The signals that survived the search were tuned to a
low volatility, trending sub period. When volatility clustering returned, the
volatility filter that had looked protective in sample became a lag that
entered late and exited later.

## Deflated Sharpe ratio

The deflated Sharpe ratio[^dsr] corrects the observed Sharpe for the number of
trials, the length of the sample, and the non-normality of returns. Writing
$\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}$ for the estimate and $\mathrm{SR}_0$ for the expected
maximum under the null of no skill, the deflated statistic is

$$
\mathrm{DSR} = \Phi\!\left(
  \frac{(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}} - \mathrm{SR}_0)\,\sqrt{N - 1}}
       {\sqrt{\,1 - \gamma_3\,\widehat{\mathrm{SR}} + \tfrac{\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\,\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}^{\,2}}}
\right),
$$

where $N$ is the number of observations, and $\gamma_3,\gamma_4$ are the skew
and kurtosis of the returns. Plugging in the search size pushed $\mathrm{SR}_0$
high enough that the once impressive 3.1 produced a $\mathrm{DSR}$ far below any
reasonable confidence threshold. The number was screaming "noise" before a
single live trade.

## Lessons

- Count your trials. The Sharpe you report means nothing without the size of
  the search that produced it.
- Prefer fewer, theory motivated parameters over large grids. Every degree of
  freedom is a chance for noise to masquerade as signal.
- Reserve a genuinely untouched holdout, and treat the first live quarter as
  the real out of sample test.
- Treat any single instrument Sharpe above 2 as guilty until proven innocent.

[^dsr]: Bailey, D. and Lopez de Prado, M. (2014). "The Deflated Sharpe Ratio:
    Correcting for Selection Bias, Backtest Overfitting, and Non-Normality."
    *Journal of Portfolio Management*.
